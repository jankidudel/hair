If dry strands suddenly lift, spread or cling to the brush just after a pass, static may be involved. Contact between the brush and the hair can transfer electric charge, and strands carrying the same charge push away from each other. Check whether the same strands stood out before you brushed, then try fewer passes on a small section before changing brushes.

Look at when the strands lift

Timing is the most useful clue you can see. Static from the brush pass shows up right after brushing dry hair. Strands that were already standing out, or a finish that spreads during drying, point to a different step in the routine. Appearance alone cannot identify the cause of every flyaway, so use the table to decide what to check next rather than as a verdict.

Why a dry brush pass can change the finish

Static is an imbalance between positive and negative charge on an object. The Library of Congress static-electricity explainer describes how rubbing two materials together can move charge from one to the other. A brush drawn through hair is that kind of contact, repeated along every strand it touches.

A review of hair-fibre characteristics in the Brazilian Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences describes combing as producing a surface charge on hair through friction, helped by hair’s high electrical resistance. It also notes that charge leaves wet hair more easily, so hair tends to fly away more in dry conditions than humid ones. That is why the same brush can seem fine one week and lift your hair on a cold, dry day.

Those sources describe the mechanism in general. They do not show that every plastic brush causes static, or that one bristle material always prevents it. How much a particular brush and your hair charge depends on the materials, the conditions and how many passes you make, so test your own routine rather than relying on a material label.

Change one thing first

This is an observation you can try at home, not a test of a product. Work in one room, at one time, so the conditions are as close as you can make them for both sections.

  1. Choose two manageable sections of dry hair that look similar. Note how each sits before you touch it.
  2. Brush the first section the way you normally do and count the passes.
  3. Brush the second section with fewer passes, just enough to remove tangles.
  4. Compare the two straight away and again a minute later. Look for lifting, clinging to the brush and strands spreading apart.
  5. If fewer passes look calmer, keep the shorter routine. If both look the same and you already own a suitable alternative brush or comb, repeat the comparison with it under similar conditions.

Change only the number of passes first, because it costs nothing and does not depend on buying anything. If you also change the brush, the product and the room at once, you will not know which change helped. Record what you tried so the next comparison starts from the same point.

Keep the finish you want

If you wear a dry curl or wave pattern, brushing it can separate the groups you were trying to keep, and the lift you see may be the pattern breaking up rather than static. In that case brush before styling rather than after; the curly-hair brush guide separates preparation tools from finishing ones.

If the lift only appears after a hot-air brush or a blow-dry, the drying step is the place to adjust, not the dry brush pass. The hot-air brush frizz guide and blowout troubleshooting own those cases.

With the brush you already have, the next step is simple: use fewer, slower passes on dry hair, stop once the tangles are out, and smooth the surface with your palm rather than repeating the brush. FAQ

Is static after brushing caused by the brush material?

Contact between brush and hair can transfer charge, but the sources here do not show that one material always causes it or always prevents it. Test fewer passes on your own hair before changing brushes.

Why is it worse in winter?

Charge leaves hair more slowly when it is dry, and dry indoor or outdoor air is common in colder months. The same brushing can therefore look different from one week to the next.

Are all flyaways static?

No. Strands that were already raised before brushing, or a finish that spreads during drying, point to another step. Look at when the lift appears before deciding what to change.
