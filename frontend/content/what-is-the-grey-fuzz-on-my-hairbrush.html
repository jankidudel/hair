Grey fuzz on a hairbrush is usually a mixture of lint, dust, shed skin, natural oil and styling-product residue—not something a brush can identify from a brush. It gathers where loose hair catches around the base of the bristles.

Why it builds up

It tends to appear faster when a brush is used with styling products or dry shampoo, or left uncovered in a dusty room. The practical answer is not to scrub aggressively: first lift out hair, then clean the surface in a way that suits the material.

What you see Practical response
Soft grey lint around intact bristles Lift hair, then wipe or clean appropriately
Residue packed near the base Use a brush-cleaning tool or comb tail gently
Bent pins, split cushion or missing bristles Consider replacing the brush

Remove it safely

Start at the outer edge with fingers, a comb tail or a cleaning tool; do not cut through bristles or lever hard against a cushion. For the full material-by-material method, read how to clean a hairbrush without damaging it. Natural bristle, wood-backed and cushioned brushes need less water than a washable plastic brush.

Reduce recurrence

Remove trapped hair when you notice it, let a cleaned brush dry before putting it away, and store it away from splashes and dust where practical. The boar bristle versus nylon guide explains why material changes the care routine.

When to replace the brush

Cleaning cannot restore a split cushion, sharp pins, missing bristles or a barrel that no longer turns smoothly. If the brush catches, feels uncomfortable or no longer gives enough control, replacement is the practical choice.

A simple cleaning sequence

Work when the brush is dry and easy to see. Lift the loose hair from the edge toward the centre, then use a comb tail or purpose-made cleaning tool to ease out compacted lint. Avoid pulling one large clump straight through the pins: it can bend them or tug at a cushion. Once the loose material is removed, wipe the back and handle with a soft cloth and use only the amount of moisture the brush material permits.

Let the brush dry in an airy place before returning it to a drawer or bag. For a wooden back, natural bristles or a cushioned pad, a barely damp cloth is often the more cautious option; follow the maker’s care instructions where they differ. A washable plastic brush may tolerate a fuller clean, but it still benefits from drying bristle-side down so water does not sit near the base.

Make the routine easier

Keep a small comb tail near the place you style your hair and remove trapped strands before they become a tight mat. If you use dry shampoo, finishing spray or creams, check the brush more often rather than waiting for a thick layer to form. Storing a clean, dry brush in a drawer, pouch or covered tray can also reduce household dust settling between uses. These are ordinary housekeeping steps for keeping a styling tool ready to use.
