Sectioning gives each part of a routine a clear working area. The useful plan is one you can follow without collecting neighbouring strands, losing your place or repeatedly releasing hair you have already finished.

Name the task before making the partings

A section for working through a tangle is not automatically the right section for a brush-styling pass. Decide what the next movement needs: access to the ends, room to guide a brush, or an area you can cover with a product. This keeps the section plan connected to the task instead of copying a diagram made for a different routine.

Tangle Teezer’s UK blow-dry tutorial describes sectioning and clipping unused hair away from the working area. That supports the organisation step; it does not establish one section count or a heat setting for all tools.

Separate, park, work and release

  1. Find the area you can comfortably reach and inspect.
  2. Separate it with your fingers or a suitable comb, without forcing through a catch.
  3. Park unused lengths with a fastening that holds them without pulling.
  4. Keep the fastener and unused hair clear of the movement you plan to make.
  5. Complete that working area, then release the next part deliberately.

Use a mirror to check the back if you cannot see where the boundaries meet. The parked area should help you find the next section, rather than conceal a bundle you will have to rediscover later. If you cannot keep the boundary clear, divide the area again before starting the tool movement.

Keep working sections different from storage bundles

A broad upper bundle may be convenient to park, while the area released from it needs to be smaller. There is no contradiction: one holds unused hair and the other gives access for the next movement. Release part of the parked bundle instead of assuming that everything held by one clip must be worked at once.

This also explains why buying a larger clip does not necessarily solve a crowded brush pass. The clip may already park the hair correctly. Review the amount you release and the route your hands take before replacing an accessory. The sectioning-clip selection guide owns jaw shape and mechanism choices.

Start with a short trial area rather than committing the entire head to a complicated grid. Notice whether you can put the tool down, find your place again and continue. If that is difficult, simplify the boundaries. A repeatable plan is more useful than matching somebody else’s number of clips.

Record the part that made the routine easier

For the next session, remember where you started and how you identified finished areas. You might work through one side before the other, or release a lower layer from a parked upper bundle. These are examples of organisation, not a mandatory order. Keep the sequence that lets you reach each area without straining or repeatedly undoing previous work.

When an appliance is involved, first check its starting-state, accessory and product restrictions. A clear section does not override those instructions. If you are still deciding which stages belong in the routine, use hair styling product order before adding another step.

FAQ

How many sections should I make?

Use enough to keep the working area accessible and separate. A fixed number cannot account for every tool, task and amount of hair.

Do I need a tail comb?

Not automatically. Fingers may make a usable boundary; a suitable comb can help when you want a more precise part.

Should one clip equal one styling section?

No. A clip can park a broad bundle while you release smaller working areas from it.
