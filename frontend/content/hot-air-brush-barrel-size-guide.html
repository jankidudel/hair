A hot-air-brush size is useful only when you know what was measured. A round core, an oval head and the outside span of bristles can all carry a size label. Compare those definitions before using a number to choose a tool for your routine.

Quick answer

Record head shape, measurement definition, working length and the section you need to reach. Keep missing dimensions unknown. Compare an alternative against the part of your routine that fails, rather than choosing a universal small, medium or large category.

Identify the measurement first

Table: swipe sideways on a small screen, or focus and use arrow keys.

Measurement What it describes What it does not establish
Round core diameter Distance across a cylindrical core The full span of its bristles
Oval long and short axes Two different crosswise spans An equivalent single round diameter
Outside bristle span The envelope around the bristles The exact core curve or usable length
Working-head length Space along the head The total appliance or handle length
Overall tool length The complete body dimension How much working surface accepts a section

If a listing says only head size, retain that wording and ask what the number means. Do not relabel it as a measured diameter. Unit conversion changes the unit, not the definition: converting inches to millimetres cannot make two unlike measurements equivalent.

Keep shape and size as separate questions

A circular cross-section and an oval cross-section present different curves. Either can also be physically large or small. A numerical comparison is incomplete when one source gives an oval label and the other describes a round head without clarifying whether bristles are included.

For two US oval brushes, compare Drybar Double Shot with Revlon One-Step Original. The Hot Tools Black Gold and 24K Gold comparison shows what two US listings mean by an oval size label and what their pages leave unstated.

Inspect the shortest section and the longest path

Choose the shortest piece you actually intend to style. Ask whether the head and body can enter its working area, reach it and leave without collecting adjacent hair. A different head may help, but a narrower working section or another permitted method may already solve the access question.

Then choose the longest path you regularly guide. Inspect how the whole head would travel and how the length would leave it. Working space along the head is separate from the crosswise curve. Do not assume a wider head fixes crowding along its length or that a complete wrap is required for every tool.

Use observations from the tool you already own

Write one concrete observation: the body blocks a lower layer, a face-framing piece misses the curve, or the head repeatedly gathers a neighbouring section. Keep that observation separate from a product's dimension. It describes your routine and does not establish why every user would prefer another head.

Where possible, compare a cool, unplugged display tool in the intended grip. Do not turn a fit inspection into an unsupervised heated trial. Actual styling still follows the exact appliance and product instructions, including the permitted starting state.

A missing measurement remains an open question, and an awkward access observation identifies something to investigate rather than automatically requiring a purchase.

If the current tool reaches, guides and releases the relevant section, retain that as the baseline. A second head needs a specific reason: a defined dimension, body route or control change you want to inspect. More attachments and a larger size label do not create that reason on their own.
