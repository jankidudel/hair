Watts describe the dryer’s rated electrical input. Volts and hertz describe the supply it is designed to use. When comparing dryers for a routine, also check the controls, included attachments and what any published weight includes. Use the label decoder to read your dryer and the calculator to estimate running cost.

Read the label and the product listing

The rating label on the dryer carries the electrical figures; the product listing or manual carries the rest. The example values in the first two rows are an illustrative label, not a specific model: a rating plate reading “220–240 V ~ 50/60 Hz 2000 W”.

What you see What it tells you What to check next
Rated watts, for example “2000 W” The maximum electrical input the appliance is rated for. It is the figure the running-cost calculator uses. Not airflow or drying speed. If drying is slow, use the slow-drying checks instead of comparing watts.

Heat and speed controls How many separate heat and airflow choices you get, and whether a cool shot is a button you hold or a setting that stays on. Whether you can lower airflow without losing heat control. The hair dryer chooser matches controls to routines.
Included attachments listed in the box The exact concentrator, diffuser or comb supplied with that package. A part shown elsewhere on the page may be sold separately. Match each part to a job you repeat, using the buying paths in the chooser.
Published weight A number only becomes comparable when you know what was weighed: body alone, or with cord and attachment. Look for wording such as “without cable”. If the basis is not stated, compare that number with caution.

Worked example: what a 2,000 W dryer uses

Electricity use depends on power and time together. Multiply the power in kilowatts by the hours it runs to get kilowatt-hours (kWh), then multiply by your unit rate:

  • kWh per use = watts ÷ 1,000 × minutes ÷ 60
  • Cost per use = kWh per use × price per kWh
  • Weekly cost = cost per use × uses per week
  • 52-week estimate = weekly cost × 52

A 2,000 W dryer running for 10 minutes uses 2 × 10 ÷ 60 = 0.333 kWh. At an illustrative £0.25 per kWh, that is about £0.083 (8.3p) per use. Five uses a week comes to about £0.42 a week, or about £21.67 over 52 weeks. If your rate is in pence or cents, divide it by 100 first. The 25p rate is an example for the arithmetic, not today's tariff: use the unit rate on your own bill.

The calculation assumes the dryer draws its full rated power for the whole time. The rating on the label is a maximum for the appliance, not a measurement of every heat and speed setting, so a session on lower settings can use less. If you have measured the average power with a plug-in energy monitor, enter that figure instead. The method follows the watts × time × unit rate arithmetic used in National Energy Action's appliance-cost explanation; its 2022 tariff is historical and is not used as a default here.

Watts versus kWh

Watts (W) are a rate: how much power the dryer draws at a moment. Kilowatt- hours (kWh) are an amount: the energy used over time, and the unit your electricity bill charges for. A 1,000 W appliance running for one hour uses 1 kWh. A 2,000 W dryer reaches 1 kWh after 30 minutes of running at full power.

Label What it answers What it cannot answer alone
Watts (W) Rated electrical power draw Drying time, airflow at the brush, or comfort
Kilowatt-hours (kWh) Energy used over a stated time Cost, until you multiply by your own unit rate
Volts (V) Supply range the exact appliance accepts Whether your plug fits or an adapter has adequate rating
Heat and speed controls Available adjustments on that model Which combination will suit every hair type

1,600 W vs 2,000 W: which uses less?

Per minute at full power, the 1,600 W rating draws 20% less than the 2,000 W rating. Per finished blow-dry, the answer depends on how long each dryer runs. Ten minutes at 1,600 W uses 0.267 kWh; ten minutes at 2,000 W uses 0.333 kWh. If the lower-rated dryer needed 13 minutes for the same routine, it would use about 0.347 kWh, slightly more. A lower rating therefore does not always mean a cheaper blow-dry. Time your own routine before comparing.

The rating also does not tell you which dryer will feel faster. Motor design, airflow path, nozzle shape and the selected heat and speed setting all affect drying, and two dryers with similar wattage can behave very differently. When a maker publishes airflow or motor information, use it as an extra comparison point rather than reading the watt figure as a performance score. For a named example of why the input figure needs context, compare Parlux Alyon and Digitalyon: their published wattage and airflow figures remain separate from the controls, selected pack and result in your own routine.

How to read wattage when choosing a dryer

There is no universal target. The ranges below are a practical way to read a listing; they are categories to investigate, not promises of performance.

Wattage shown What it may suggest What to check next
Under 1,400 W Often a compact or travel-focused dryer, where lower weight and packability matter. Compare airflow, heat and speed options, folded size and voltage marking.
1,400–1,800 W A common middle range for everyday home dryers. Compare controls, attachments, weight and cable length.
Over 1,800 W Often a full-size, power-focused home dryer. Not a guarantee of stronger airflow or faster drying. Check airflow information, heat control, noise, weight and handling.
Not listed The number may be on the rating label or in the manual instead. Find the manufacturer specification before using wattage as a deciding factor.

If you mostly dry at home, start with the routine you want, then compare controls, the attachments you will actually use, weight and cable length. A well-shaped concentrator can make directed drying easier, and a comfortable weight can matter more than a small specification difference if you dry your hair often. If you want a light or compact dryer, a lower wattage may be the right trade-off; check the airflow and settings so you know what you are giving up. Voltage and travel are separate from wattage

The appliance rating also gives an input voltage and sometimes frequency. Compare that exact input with an authoritative description of the destination supply, then check plug shape, earthing and the adapter's own maximum rating. A plug adapter does not change voltage or frequency, as Electrical Safety First explains.

As a real model contrast, Conair lists automatic dual voltage for its US DigitalAIRE 2.0, while Dyson's Supersonic Travel HD19 owner guide specifies automatic 100–240 V operation and a suitably rated adapter. Neither statement applies to every Conair or Dyson dryer. Check your own rating label, any selector instructions, the destination supply and the plug separately. Sources checked 4 October 2026. If a label, manual or destination rating is missing, ask the manufacturer before using the dryer abroad. A quick label check before you buy

  1. The printed wattage, if running cost or a like-for-like comparison matters to you.
  2. The printed voltage range, especially for travel.
  3. Whether the dryer has the heat and speed controls you expect.
  4. Which attachments are included and whether you will use them.
  5. The listed weight, dimensions and cable length for the way you will use it.

When drying feels slower than expected

Read wattage as a label detail rather than a stopwatch prediction. Compare starting dampness, section width, attachment and airflow setting across two ordinary sessions. A sudden change in airflow calls for an accessible filter check under the exact model instructions, while a dryer that has always felt slow may call for a routine adjustment.
